# Information quality

The reference is not new in written culture.
A book has references,
but a book has no verification and no index behind them.
There is no way to confirm a reference, and no way to collect all references to one source.
The scientific journal added both
— a reviewer who is responsible for verification, and a citation index.
But it did this expensively and slowly:
review and publication are costly,
and issues are bound to time.
Cybergraph makes each of these capabilities stronger
and, in addition, democratizes writing itself
— anyone can publish.

Reuse happens at the level of an individual claim, not of a file (see "Changing scale").
Verification becomes collective, measurable, and continuous,
with a number of participants that manual review cannot reach (see "Measurable consensus").
A corpus is also no longer locked in its medium.
Discussions from letters, forums, and oral traditions are ported into the graph
and verified in it again (see "Authorship").

Together, this makes it possible to process the accumulated corpus again.
For example, one can evaluate quantitatively what Buddhism and Advaita have in common.
The evaluation finds where they call the same phenomenon by different names (`same`) and where they differ.
One can do this without the bottleneck of a single expert:
the evaluation accumulates from many signers.
After such knowledge is recorded, it stays addressable,
and so it is available to process further.
When new facts appear,
the corpus is processed again from the start
— now by agents (see "Agents over the graph").
Concepts are then connected to quantitative metrics,
and this improves discovery (the search for relevant information).
The graph does not compute "the same thing said differently" as similarity,
but carries it as a judgment
— the same `same` edge.
Someone states the equivalence,
so, like any edge, it can be denied (`deny_edge`) and measured by consensus.
