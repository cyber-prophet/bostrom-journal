### Cyberlink and cybergraph [~](particles/QmR4a3KUUxjHtQECyro1f5zT9EqWX3LWcWH32brj4XQWXj.md)

The CID (content identifier) of any file can be computed:
it is an IPFS hash of the bytes of the file.
A file becomes a *particle* of the cybergraph
when its CID is used in an edge of a cyberlink.
A particle is one name for two things:
a vertex of the graph and the bytes of the file under its CID.
A particle carries meaning:
the meaning occurs in the reader when the reader reads the content of the particle.
For this issue of the journal, particles are text only:
human-readable text or machine-readable data structures that are serialized in JSON.

A cyberlink is a statement by a signer:
"these two particles are connected".
A signer is an agent (for example, a human, an AI) that created and signed the cyberlink.
Cybergraph stores the connection and also the signer that states it,
but it does not store the meaning.
A book stores only the text that is printed on paper,
and the meaning occurs in the reader.
In the same way, here interpretation produces the meaning of the statement at read time (see "Conventions").

Cybergraph is a data structure that is made of cyberlinks.
Each cyberlink is a signed record of this form:

```
{
    cid_from: Qm1
    cid_to: Qm2
    signer: Qm3
}
```

`cid_from` and `cid_to` are the CIDs of the two particles that are connected.
The pair `cid_from → cid_to` is an *edge* of the cybergraph, directed from `cid_from` to `cid_to`.
The particles are its vertices.
The `signer` field is the CID of the public key of the signer (Qm3),
so the signer can be referenced like any CID.
The key of the signer becomes a particle
when this CID stands in an edge.
The appendix "Cybergraph format" defines the exact encoding.

All three fields
— `cid_from`, `cid_to`, and `signer` —
are plain text strings.
Thus, we can serialize them into a JSON record in canonical form and compute the IPFS hash of the bytes of this record.
This hash is a derived CID.
Here and below, a record in the place of a CID means the CID of the canonical bytes of the record.
The records in the text are not the bytes themselves:
the appendix defines the bytes.
The hash of the record `{"cid_from":Qm1,"cid_to":Qm2}` is the *edge address*.
It contains no signer,
so it is the same for everyone who connected the same particles.
It addresses *what* is stated.
The hash of the record `{"cid_from":Qm1,"cid_to":Qm2,"signer":Qm3}` is the derived CID of the cyberlink.
It addresses the act of a specific signer
— *who* states it.
A signer can use both addresses in other cyberlinks.
Thus, a signer can use other cyberlinks to issue instructions about how to interpret edges and specific cyberlinks.
Before such use, a derived CID is only an address.
Its preimage, a record such as `{"cid_from":Qm1,"cid_to":Qm2}`, becomes a particle like any file
when this CID stands in an edge.

In this issue of the journal, we design and consider possible approaches.
These approaches are open conventions to encode and interpret instructions to process the cybergraph.
What we design here is only the result of thought exercises, and it is not tested in practice yet.
Implementation will show what to change in the conventions.
But that is a task for the future.

### Fundamentals [~](particles/QmPCdtP2ZJFQNdw4zKKY7WUwyHamCFjhQLqujM2ToiTbYR.md)

The definition in the chapter "Cyberlink and cybergraph" does not mention a blockchain.
The triple and the derived CIDs are defined on top of IPFS hashes.
This is the *format* of Cybergraph, and it is suitable for any medium
where records are published with a signature.
Cybergraph was first implemented on the Bostrom blockchain.

In Bostrom, on cyb.ai, and in the first issue of the journal, a signer has another name: *neuron*.

A Bostrom cyberlink carries the same triple as the format:
`cid_from` and `cid_to` are in the record,
and the transaction signature identifies the signer.
Only the encoding of the signer is different.
In Bostrom, it is a bostrom1… address (a blockchain-specific hash of the same key).
In the format, it is the CID of the public key (see the appendix "Cybergraph format").

In addition to the triple, the blockchain adds only publication metadata.
Bostrom is a blockchain that is built on the Cosmos SDK,
so every cyberlink is in a transaction.
Thus, two more facts can be read from the raw data of the blockchain:

- height and timestamp — when the cyberlink was published
- account sequence — the signer's own transaction counter, which runs without gaps

Each transaction with cyberlinks has a signature from the key of its signer,
and validators sign the block that carries the transaction.
Anyone can verify these signatures.
And, with no trust in the data provider,
anyone can make sure that a specific signer published a specific cyberlink at a known time.
The proof also applies to the content:
the content determines its CID.
Thus, a cyberlink in a block is proof
that the content of both particles already existed at the moment of publication.
Anyone who has the file computes its CID and compares it with the CID in the cyberlink.
Because the account sequence runs without gaps,
every transaction of the signer can be read,
so no cyberlink of the signer stays hidden.

### Novelty [~](particles/QmWbEBhd4J2n7NexCnNb15mcnps3gDc3c9srNVdQaFJGrk.md)

Cybergraph adds no new cryptographic mechanisms.
It combines known mechanisms
— content addressing, signed logs, reification, signed statements, links to content and visible reuse —
and adds one move.
The *connection* itself gets a content address
— in the cybergraph, this connection is the edge —
and so it becomes a shared operand.
The edge address is not negotiated. It is computed with one format (see the appendix).
Below, we show where similar mechanisms are already in use (Nostr uses almost all of them at once), and what is different.

**Content addressing: IPFS/IPLD and Git.**
"The name is the hash of the content" is the base of the whole construction (the CID).
This idea is not new.
Git addresses commits, trees, and blobs by the hash of their content,
and so makes a Merkle DAG.
IPFS/IPLD extends this to an addressable graph of any data,
and Cybergraph technically stands on this graph.
The new part here is not the addressing of objects. It is the addressing of the connections between them (see below).

**Signed logs without trust: Secure Scuttlebutt and Certificate Transparency.**
The argument of "Fundamentals"
— an account sequence without gaps makes a signer's record provably complete —
is a property of an append-only log.
Secure Scuttlebutt [Tarr et al., 2019] gives each participant a signed log with sequence numbers.
A gap is visible,
and nobody can remove entries from a replica without detection
— exactly as here.
Certificate Transparency [RFC 6962] builds a public Merkle log of certificates.
Any auditor verifies this log without trust in the publisher of the log.
A certificate that was issued falsely is detected
exactly because nobody can rewrite the log after the fact.
Cybergraph inherits this property:
verifiable completeness without trust in the data provider.
But it moves the support of the property from each participant's separate log (SSB) and a centralized operator (CT) to blockchain consensus and transaction order.

**Signed events and the social graph: Nostr.**
Nostr is the closest system in active use.
A Nostr event is `{id, pubkey, tags, content, sig}`.
In it, `id` is the sha256 of the serialized event,
and tags refer to other events and keys.
On top of this, reactions and reposts build a social graph of signed references.
Almost everything matches:
a signature by a key, an address derived from content, edges between messages.
The difference is in the status of the edge.
In Nostr, a tag is a reference *inside* an event, and it is the metadata of one author.
The connection has no deterministic address of its own that is the same for everyone.
Suppose that someone wants to target the connection of another author with a confirmation, a denial, or an argument.
Then a separate convention would first have to define what counts as a connection, and give it a computable name.
In Cybergraph, a record carries only the connection and the signer,
and `{"cid_from":A,"cid_to":B}` is a standalone address.
The connection is addressable before, and apart from, whoever published it.
So the recursion of conventions (confirm an edge, deny a confirmation, bring an argument against an argument) is uniform.
Also, Nostr relays give no shared order.
Here, the blockchain gives this order.

**Reification and identity: RDF.**
The method "make a statement into an object to make statements about it" is RDF reification.
In it, a triple (subject, predicate, object) is represented as an `rdf:Statement` resource,
and new triples can be made about this resource.
RDF-star [W3C, 2021] makes the statement a term of the language itself.
`same` directly matches `owl:sameAs`.
The difference is in the origin of the identity of the reified connection.
In classical reification, an issued IRI names the resource of the statement.
If two authors want to speak about the same statement,
they must negotiate the name.
RDF-star removes this need structurally:
the identity of a quoted triple comes from its components.
But the components stay IRIs, which are issued names.
So the negotiation moves to their level,
and nothing ties the name to the content behind the IRI.
In Cybergraph, the components are CIDs.
The edge address is *computed*
— `{"cid_from":A,"cid_to":B}` —
so it is one for everyone
and cryptographically tied to the content of A and B.
This is the single new move:
content addressing applied to the edge.

**Signed statements and trust: PGP Web of Trust and Verifiable Credentials.**
Authorship as a cyberlink (`{"about":X,"op":"author_particle"} → P`) and the signature under every cyberlink belong to the family "make implicit trust explicit and verifiable".
PGP Web of Trust [Zimmermann] replaces the central certificate authority with a network of mutual key–identity signatures.
W3C Verifiable Credentials [W3C VC] is a standard for a statement
in which an issuer signs a declaration about a subject.
A verifier checks the signature of this statement.
The porting of discussions from other mediums (the section "Authorship") is the same step
that separates a VC from a bare From header.
In this step, attribution changes from a string into a signed addressable statement.
The difference is in the economy of primitives.
The issuer/holder/subject roles and the revocation registries of VC, like the trust layers of WoT,
reduce here to cyberlinks plus `deny_edge`/`deny_link`.
The weight of a signer stays a read policy, not a part of the protocol.

**Links to content and visible reuse: Xanadu.**
The oldest description of the program is Project Xanadu [Nelson, 2000],
the first hypertext project, started in 1960.
Its requirements for electronic literature read as a list of the properties of Cybergraph:
links that do not break when versions change, and *transclusion*.
Transclusion is the same content, recognizably present in more than one place, with the visible origin of every excerpt.
The xanalogical structure distinguishes exactly two kinds of connection
— between different things (content link) and between the same thing (transclusion).
The connection is not embedded in the document,
but it is placed over the document from outside, and it targets content, not a place.
Nelson compared the HTML link to a shop window:
the window shows what is on display at the address now.
The difference is in the origin of identity.
In Nelson's design, content gets an address at registration:
every published character gets a permanent address.
So identity is traceable only through the operations of the system itself.
The same text, written independently, gets a different address.
This design could work only as a separate worldwide infrastructure.
It was never built:
in six decades, there were prototypes, demos, and open code (Udanax, 1999), but no network that worked.
Cybergraph gets the Xanadu program for free and with technical elegance.
The address is computed from the content,
so identical content is one particle,
no matter who wrote it and when.
A link to a CID cannot break.
Transclusion is built into the addressing.
And the `quote_particle` convention makes the origin of an excerpt visible
— a property of the address, not a separate system.

### Conventions [~](particles/QmSizUFV6gkkzfmLYE4LDaR3S9rJsTkktWAUeVGUNwYEjE.md)

A published edge `A → B` has an address
— `{"cid_from":A,"cid_to":B}`, the CID of a record of A and B.
A signer can refer to it as to any other CID.

This lets us make derived CIDs.
When an edge has an address,
we can refer to it and write it together with other CIDs into the fields of a record. We can also build structures that refer to edges
— and to edges about edges.

In particular, we use this to build, on top of the raw graph, instructions to process it.
To issue an instruction, a signer puts a special CID
— such as `{"op":"deny_edge"}` or `{"about":A,"op":"quote_particle"}` —
in the source of an ordinary edge.
In the record, `op` is the convention word
and `about` is the subject of the instruction.
If the subject is an edge, the address of the edge identifies it.
Each such use is itself an edge with an address,
so each instruction can in turn be confirmed or denied with the same conventions.
This recursion makes the set of conventions open.
The suffix of a convention word names the kind of its subject.
`_particle` names a file, `_edge` names an edge, `_link` names a cyberlink.
A bare word (`pro`, `con`, `question`, `same`) names any CID.
Thus a mistake such as `agree_particle` with an edge address is visible in the word itself.
An evaluation (`deny_edge`, `agree_particle`) has no `about`:
the source is fixed,
and the subject is in the target.

We build instructions from the elements of the graph itself. This is a kind of *homoiconicity*
(a property known from programming languages in which code and data have one representation).
Here, that representation is the CID:
an instruction is made of the same CIDs and cyberlinks as the graph that it processes.
Thus a program that processes the graph is inside it.
This is also true at any scale.
At any time, the whole cybergraph can be fixed as a file
whose address is a CID that is computed over it.

A content address is computed from the content itself:
the same content always gives the same CID.
Thus the same content is the same particle, no matter who writes it or when.
The address is a reference and an identity at the same time.
Identical content is not stored twice.
The graph does not separate these two properties
— one write operation gives both.

Cybergraph is a general-purpose data structure.
It is suitable for many kinds of tasks, and it is not tied to a blockchain (see the format in "Fundamentals").
In addition, the implementation in Bostrom is permissionless.
Anyone who has a key pair can write to it,
and anyone can read it and process it in their own way.
There is no gatekeeper here that decides who can write and what counts as valid.
The blockchain checks only the technical correctness of a transaction
— signature and order —
not the meaning or the validity of what is written.
Cryptography gives the protection.
Each signer keeps control of their own records,
and what is written is immutable.
Thus the raw graph contains only CIDs and cyberlinks
and has no built-in meaning.
Meaning is produced at read time
— an application follows the conventions that it chooses for itself.

Conventions also do not have to be universal.
Consumers of the graph come from different fields and invent mechanics for their own tasks.
For a mechanic to work in a collective,
the collective must agree on it.
Collaborative work on information is based on this:
a shared immutable foundation
— and freely chosen consensus mechanics on top of it, dynamic ones too.

The sections below give basic examples of such conventions
— how to *encode* and how to *interpret* instructions to process the cybergraph.
(`hash("text")` is the CID of the bytes of the text itself.)

#### Confirming an edge and a cyberlink [~](particles/QmPtMMiAY2e1dheUZw3JqYHo9trStfSYeY3NuAXn934ymu.md)

A signer can support a statement in two ways:
make it their own, or confirm a statement of another signer.

The first way: simply repeat the edge.

Unlike `deny_edge`/`update_edge`/`quote_particle`, a repetition does not need a special CID in the source:
it is simply the same edge that another signer publishes again.
There is no convention here at all
— a repetition has no record that goes into the source.
Also, a repetition says more than an evaluation:
the signer personally makes the same statement.

But a signer can support a statement and not make it their own:

```
A - CID
B - CID
signer - the CID of the signer of the cyberlink being confirmed

{"op":"confirm_edge"} → {"cid_from":A,"cid_to":B}                  # confirming the edge
{"op":"confirm_link"} → {"cid_from":A,"cid_to":B,"signer":signer}  # confirming a specific cyberlink
```

Confirmation has two targets, and thus two semantics.
Each of them has its own word, and the words mirror `deny_edge`/`deny_link` (see "Denying an edge and a cyberlink"):
- `confirm_edge` → `{"cid_from":A,"cid_to":B}` — confirms the statement `A → B` itself:
  the statement is true, no matter who published it
- `confirm_link` → `{"cid_from":A,"cid_to":B,"signer":signer}` — confirms a specific cyberlink of a specific signer:
  the record is valid
  — for example, it is not spam —
  with no opinion about the statement itself

The word itself names its subject.
A reader who filters instructions only by the word
gets the confirmations of edges separately from the confirmations of cyberlinks.
The reader does not parse the target of each edge.
As with `deny_edge`/`deny_link`, the sources `{"op":"confirm_edge"}` and `{"op":"confirm_link"}` are fixed CIDs:
what they point to is what is confirmed.

Repetition and `confirm_edge` are different signals,
and work distinguishes them.
Repetition needs work:
the archivist compared the copies of the source, the agent repeated the computation, the reader checked the substring of the quote.
Only then does the signer state the same thing.
`confirm_edge` is support from a signer who did not do the work.
It relies on the one who did the work.
A monk who did not personally hear the words from the Buddha cannot repeat the edge "Thus have I heard".
Such a repetition is a false claim,
but the monk has the right to confirm the edge.
Aggregation counts repetitions and confirmations separately (see "Measurable consensus").

Where a signer cannot do the work personally,
only confirmation remains.
This is the case with `update_edge`:
`{"about":{"cid_from":A,"cid_to":B},"op":"update_edge"}` is one source for everyone who proposes an update (see "Updating a previously published edge").
But C itself is the proposal of its author,
and there is nothing from which to derive it again.
If a signer repeats the update of another signer, the signer claims that they independently invented the same replacement.
If the signer confirms it, the signer accepts it.
Thus the reader uses confirmations, not repetitions, to distinguish the versions that compete.

The signature proves the authenticity of a record (see "Fundamentals"),
and there is no reason to certify it:
`confirm_link` is a judgment about validity.
Its direct use is a counterweight.
Some signers deny a cyberlink as spam and other signers confirm it as valid,
and both sides are visible next to each other.

#### Denying an edge and a cyberlink [~](particles/QmVyrZSBE2RE8M1XMFNeu8ZG4pZsfzkgiu2wCP9yKa411k.md)

```
A - CID
B - CID
signer - the CID of the signer of the denied cyberlink

{"op":"deny_edge"} → {"cid_from":A,"cid_to":B}                  # denying the edge
{"op":"deny_link"} → {"cid_from":A,"cid_to":B,"signer":signer}  # denying a specific cyberlink
```

Denial has two targets, and thus two semantics.
Each of them has its own word:
- `deny_edge` → `{"cid_from":A,"cid_to":B}` — denies the statement `A → B` itself:
  the statement is false, no matter who published it
- `deny_link` → `{"cid_from":A,"cid_to":B,"signer":signer}` — denies a specific cyberlink of a specific signer:
  the record is invalid
  — for example, as spam —
  with no opinion about the statement itself

Unlike `update_edge` and `quote_particle`, which have their operand in the `about` of the source,
`{"op":"deny_edge"}` and `{"op":"deny_link"}` are fixed CIDs.
What they point to is what is denied.

#### Updating a previously published edge [~](particles/QmSdVXrgpAHrFVRewv11EiPnRQiX2rPg2zkb5CjLb6NY7x.md)

```
A - a CID from the original edge
B - the other CID from the original edge
C - a new CID replacing B

A → B                                                        # the original edge
A → C                                                        # auxiliary edge — C is reachable from A (optional)
{"about":{"cid_from":A,"cid_to":B},"op":"update_edge"} → C   # authoritative — marks C as an update of the edge
```

Each new update comes from the same CID `{"about":{"cid_from":A,"cid_to":B},"op":"update_edge"}`,
so an update can itself be updated.
The latest edge from this source is the current version.
The source is the same for everyone,
so anyone can publish an update.
Whose updates to accept
— for example, only the updates of the signer of the original cyberlink —
is a decision of the read policy.
Like any edge, the update edge itself has an address
— another signer can confirm `{"about":{"cid_from":A,"cid_to":B},"op":"update_edge"} → C` with `confirm_edge` or deny it with `deny_edge`.

#### Quotes [~](particles/QmVc6hB4YerHQc2mfvCSyqt3pqcCbJo4uSRLoa3Ljy82oS.md)

```
A - CID
B - CID, content(B) is a substring of content(A) (independently verifiable)

{"about":A,"op":"quote_particle"} → B       # "B is a faithful excerpt from A"
```

When another signer repeats a quote edge,
the repetition states that the excerpt is faithful.
`{"op":"deny_edge"} → {"cid_from":{"about":A,"op":"quote_particle"},"cid_to":B}` denies the quote edge and states that the excerpt misleads out of context.

#### Agreement and disagreement with content [~](particles/QmRshVfhfQK2Ff1iuTHkS2xmBLzfAF4Qb8yHmJearyxski.md)

```
B - the CID of the content being evaluated

{"op":"agree_particle"} → B       # "claim B is true"
{"op":"disagree_particle"} → B    # "claim B is false"
```

Repetition, `confirm_edge` and `deny_edge` work with edges:
repetition states the same edge, `confirm_edge` confirms it, and `deny_edge` denies it.
They do not apply to standalone content:
a bare CID B has no edge that a signer can repeat, confirm or deny.
With the `agree_particle`/`disagree_particle` pair, an opinion about content gets its own edges.
From there, these edges aggregate like all other edges.
The cyberlinks of different signers with one edge combine into a support level (see "Measurable consensus").

The `agree_particle`/`disagree_particle` pair and the `confirm_edge`/`deny_edge` and `confirm_link`/`deny_link` pairs are different axes,
and their subjects differ.
`agree_particle`/`disagree_particle` target the content:
claim B is true or false.
`confirm_edge`/`deny_edge` target the edge:
`A → B` is true or false;
`confirm_link`/`deny_link` target the record:
the cyberlink of a specific signer is valid or not valid.
"B is true" and "`A → B` is true" are different statements about different things.
So a signer does not express disagreement with content through `deny_link` of the agreement of another signer.
Otherwise, nobody could tell "I disagree with B" from "this agreement is invalid".
The axes combine:
`{"op":"deny_link"} → {"cid_from":{"op":"agree_particle"},"cid_to":B,"signer":signer}` denies the agreement of a specific signer
— for example, as spam —
and gives no opinion about B itself.

#### Argument for and against [~](particles/QmaLxtS4EDSDGEmiwQik4uyshSZoQktMWFxoEpPgy1riKL.md)

```
X - any CID: content, an edge, or a cyberlink
B - the CID of the argument content

{"about":X,"op":"pro"} → B      # "B is an argument for X"
{"about":X,"op":"con"} → B      # "B is an argument against X"
```

The `agree_particle`/`disagree_particle` pair records a position.
But in a live dispute, most of what the participants say is not "true/false" but *why*:
an argument, evidence, a reference to a source.
An argument is the relation "B supports X",
and it states neither that B is true nor that X is true.
A signer can think that an argument is relevant to the matter
and agree with neither the argument nor the statement.
The operand X is any CID.
Signers bring arguments to content, and also to an edge or a cyberlink
— for example, against an attribution that someone else made.

The form is the same as with `update_edge`:
the operand is in the `about` of the source.
So `{"about":X,"op":"pro"}` — the "for" side of X — is one address for all signers.
From X, a reader computes one hash and reads the outgoing edges
— that is the whole query;
the out-degree is the level of argumentation.
In the same way, `{"about":X,"op":"con"}` is the "against" side.
(The alternative `{"about":B,"op":"pro"} → X` would point the edges into the operand itself.
But the query "all arguments for X" would have to parse the source of each incoming edge.)
Like all edges, an argument is itself addressable.
Signers repeat it, deny it with `deny_edge`,
and apply `agree_particle`/`disagree_particle` to the content B
— the dispute about argument quality uses the same conventions.

#### Question and answer [~](particles/QmejhKqxPbjBZ2B4wwCvbDCwmaTEseBYXmKZUhTVHLJdMJ.md)

```
X - any CID: content, an edge, or a cyberlink
Q - the CID of the question content
R - the CID of the answer content

{"about":X,"op":"question"} → Q                                                         # "Q is a question about X"
{"about":{"cid_from":{"about":X,"op":"question"},"cid_to":Q},"op":"answer_edge"} → R    # "R is an answer to question Q about X"
```

The content of Q shows that Q is a question;
a separate edge to mark this is not necessary.
The convention carries what the content does not carry:
the address — what the question is about.
The operand X is any CID:
content, an edge, or a cyberlink.
`{"about":X,"op":"question"}` is one computable address for "questions about X":
from X, a reader computes one hash and reads the outgoing edges
— the open questions.
A repetition of the edge by another signer means "I have the same question".
The `agree_particle`/`disagree_particle` pair does not apply to Q:
the content of a question states nothing.
A question edge
— like all edges —
is denied with `deny_edge`,
and as spam with `deny_link`.

An answer attaches not to the bare Q but to the question in its context.
This is the question edge (its address is in the `about` of the source, as with `update_edge`).
The text of a question is often not complete without its context ("Is there a consensus on this?").
And one Q that signers ask about different Xs would join the answers into one shared vertex.
The edge contains no signer,
so one answer serves all who repeated the question.
The answer itself is ordinary content:
signers evaluate it with `agree_particle`/`disagree_particle`
and bring arguments to it with `pro`/`con`.
Aggregation, not an appointment, shows the best answer.

#### Authorship: porting from other mediums [~](particles/QmQoscoYLPGB12yRgjXKx5u3Gn7F3aPZ1zZbWGyTpGrS4R.md)

The signer of a cyberlink is the one who wrote the edge into the graph.
The signer does not have to be the author of the content.
This difference opens the graph to porting.
Discussions that lived in other mediums
— Usenet threads, correspondence, forums —
already have authors, dates and reply edges.
But their authorship depends on trust in servers and archives (a From header is a string, not a signature).
When an archivist ports such a discussion,
the archivist publishes the content and states the authorship with a separate edge:

```
P - the CID of the author's identifier (for example, hash("satoshin@gmx.com"))
X - the CID of the ported content

{"about":X,"op":"author_particle"} → P     # "the author of content X is P"
```

The subject is in the source, as with `pro`/`con` and `question`.
A reader of X computes one hash and reads the outgoing edges.
So the reader recognizes the attribution even if the author was not in the graph before.
Attributions that compete start at one address `{"about":X,"op":"author_particle"}`
and are visible side by side, like versions under `update_edge`.
(The mirror form `{"about":P,"op":"author_particle"} → X` would answer "all works of P" with one hash.
But the main query is the reverse one,
and with the mirror form, a reader of X cannot compute the address of an attribution from an unknown author.)

The signature under the cyberlink belongs to the archivist
and certifies the act of porting, not the authorship itself.
The trust that was implicit in the original medium
becomes an explicit addressable statement.
Then the common rules apply to it:
independent archivists who compared their own copies of the source repeat the edge.
So the attribution gets measurable consensus.
Signers deny a wrong attribution with `deny_edge`.
The ported content is immediately open to all the conventions above.
Today, a signer can extract the claims of an old thread with quote edges and sign them with `agree_particle`/`disagree_particle`.
So a dispute that started in another medium continues in the graph without loss of authorship.
The worked example below uses the same method:
"Thus have I heard" — Ananda signs the provenance of words that he did not speak.

#### Identity: another name for the same entity [~](particles/QmZWbKHqnzpwaK85qJkWFACLfKTcLJ4GmAFLYxPSeDhf2b.md)

```
P1, P2 - CIDs naming one entity

{"about":P1,"op":"same"} → P2     # "P1 and P2 are names of one entity"
```

One entity has many names in the graph:
`hash("satoshin@gmx.com")`, `hash("satoshi@vistomail.com")` and `hash("Satoshi Nakamoto")` are different CIDs.
Aggregation counts attributions under different names separately.
A `same` edge states identity (the analogue of owl:sameAs from the semantic web).
The entity is read as a cluster
— a connected component over `same` edges.
The read policy decides which edges to accept into the cluster.

Identity is symmetric in meaning,
but the edge is directed:
the mirror edge is a different pair,
and aggregation counts its support separately.
Whether to read connectivity without regard to direction, and whether to merge both directions into one statement:
the read policy decides both.
The read policy also decides the composition of the cluster.
Signers deny a false identity with `deny_edge`.
The operand is any CID, and this includes the CID of a signer.
When that signer publishes the edge `{"about":hash("Satoshi Nakamoto"),"op":"same"} → signer`, the edge is the assertion "this is me".
And ported authors (see above) connect with live participants of the graph,
who confirm or deny the assertion.
One caveat:
`same` acts at read time and does not override the format (see the appendix).
The hash puts the name into derived CIDs,
and they are not recomputed after the fact.

### Collaborative work on information [~](particles/QmYd2U8uvL5f4bANrEVogKa9oVRLA9ex6g5ZwF8uP3c6VR.md)

Cyberlinks connect particles, and through the particles they also connect signers:

```
Alice states:    A → B
Bob repeats:     A → B
Carol denies:    {"op":"deny_edge"} → {"cid_from":A,"cid_to":B}
Dave quotes:     {"about":A,"op":"quote_particle"} → B
Erin confirms:   {"op":"confirm_edge"} → {"cid_from":A,"cid_to":B}
```

The graph encodes not only "what is connected to what" but also "who believes what"
— and queries and analysis can use this data.

#### Measurable consensus [~](particles/QmVPr7TtrFqcjrwyJqCufBjdSEhRz8JuJVwYQMaoKHzcsg.md)

For any statement `A → B`:
- Count the cyberlinks of different signers with this edge → the support level:
  how many signers state the same thing
- Count the `confirm_edge`s of this edge → the support from signers who do not make the statement their own
- Count the `deny_edge`s of this edge → the dispute level
- Count the `confirm_link`s and `deny_link`s → the validity of the records (a separate axis: it says nothing about the statement)
- Weight the signers by a read policy (for example, by reputation) → the weighted consensus
- Track these results over time → the change of consensus

No central authority decides validity.
Instead:
- Signers make statements (they create cyberlinks)
- Other signers repeat, confirm, or deny them (with new cyberlinks)
- Aggregation shows the consensus
- Minority opinions stay visible (they are not censored; they are only in the minority)

```
Consensus(statement) = f(repeats, confirms, denies, signer_weights, time_decay)
```

The exact formula is a matter of read policy.
Cybergraph gives the raw data.

A signer's opinion can also change over time:
a signer can make a statement or confirm the statement of someone else,
and later deny it.
The cyberlinks of a signer have an order (see "Fundamentals").
So a reader naturally takes the latest opinion in time as the current opinion of the signer.
The earlier opinions stay visible as history.
A signer changes their opinion about a statement with `{"op":"deny_edge"} → {"cid_from":A,"cid_to":B}`.
A signer revokes their own record with `{"op":"deny_link"} → {"cid_from":A,"cid_to":B,"signer":signer}`,
where `signer` is the CID of the signer that revokes the record.

#### Changing scale: claim, file, corpus [~](particles/QmVC8Qkrj7xXT3EUH1wusowgv4TSfrpsPQHzvrkaJjvxA3.md)

It is not necessary to treat a text file A as one whole.
The file can be decomposed into the basic claims that it makes.
Each claim
(a verbatim excerpt)
is published as a separate file B, and a quote edge `{"about":A,"op":"quote_particle"} → B` connects it to the original (see "Quotes").
The decomposition is limited to verbatim text:
anyone can verify a substring.
A normalized paraphrase is built from the excerpt in a separate step
and is not part of this convention.
Now each claim has its own CID,
and it is separately open to evaluative cyberlinks
— `agree_particle` and `disagree_particle` (see "Agreement and disagreement with content").

When evaluations from different signers accumulate,
each claim collects its own statistics,
and file A as a whole gets the aggregate of these statistics.
Because a claim is addressed by its content,
its evaluations are not tied to file A.
The author of another text can include in their file a claim that already has evaluations.
The evaluation statistics then come with the claim automatically
— the CID of the claim is the same.

For the whole set of files, this opens analysis beyond the count of evaluations.
This analysis can cluster related files. It can show central tendencies and outliers that are worth an investigation. It can also find files that contradict each other or state the same thing in different words.

Together, this gives a change of scale to work on information.
One can zoom in
— from the corpus to a file, from the file to an individual claim —
and evaluate the detail. Then one can zoom out again,
and the evaluations combine into properties of the whole.
The levels do not need different mechanisms.
CIDs address the claim, the file, and the corpus,
and the same conventions apply at every scale (see "Conventions").

#### Agents over the graph: output as a reusable value [~](particles/QmTSFsBBtKF9AXZJRgpZGXhxz2hRTgoMwJAZxARiG2X4dE.md)

Agents of different types that act together need explicit rules of interaction
that each agent can verify without trust in the other agent.
Cybergraph carries such rules as conventions in the data itself
— a separate coordination layer is not necessary.

A signer does not have to be a human.
A contract or an LLM agent signs cyberlinks by the same conventions.
This changes the scale of collaborative work:
one can run different models on one corpus
and record the outputs that each model made.

A recorded output is ordinary content with its own CID,
so all the conventions above apply to it immediately.
Signers quote it (`quote_particle`), evaluate it (`agree_particle`/`disagree_particle`), and add arguments for or against it (`pro`/`con`).
But more important, it becomes input for the next output.
One model decomposed a text into claims,
another model connected them with arguments,
and a third model checked them for consistency with the corpus.
Each step reads the results of the previous steps as ready parts and does not derive them again.

Both properties of the content address work here (see "Conventions").
LLM output is expensive — tokens, latency, nondeterminism.
After an output is recorded once, it is not computed again.
To record an output, a signer puts the CID of the output into an edge from the input,
and that edge is a cache entry.
The key is the input, and the value is the output.
And the cache is shared:
the edge and the particle are visible to everyone
— no one has to repeat the work of someone else.

The difference from an ordinary computation cache is that the value can be disputed.
In a system like Nix, a build result is also addressed by content and reused,
but nobody can dispute it
— it simply is.
An output in the graph follows the common rules.
The edge that records the output is repeated, confirmed with `confirm_edge`, and denied with `deny_edge`.
The output content itself is evaluated with `agree_particle`/`disagree_particle`
— and all these cyberlinks combine into consensus (see "Measurable consensus").
Thus a computation cache becomes shared knowledge
— a unit that agents not only reuse but also check after one another.

An output is recorded with an ordinary record:
it is published as a file
and becomes a particle when its CID stands in an edge.
The signer can write provenance into the content of the output.
This provenance contains the CIDs of the processed cyberlinks and of the particles that they connect, and a description of the computation that was done.
Provenance is part of the content,
so the CID of the output fixes its inputs.
The read policy decides what to do with another signer's output:
trust the signer, measure consensus, or repeat the declared computation over the same inputs.
A signer who repeated the computation themselves publishes the same edge.
A signer who took the output as it is confirms the edge that someone else published (see "Confirming an edge and a cyberlink").
The graph shows what each vote is worth.

#### Information quality [~](particles/Qma5FKf5AQzM5uxBfdZhAawJhRBeaBN933fkJqnZ25VLgU.md)

The reference is not new in written culture.
A book has references,
but a book has no verification and no index behind them.
There is no way to confirm a reference, and no way to collect all references to one source.
The scientific journal added both
— a reviewer who is responsible for verification, and a citation index.
But it did this expensively and slowly:
review and publication are costly,
and issues are bound to time.
Cybergraph makes each of these capabilities stronger
and, in addition, democratizes writing itself
— anyone can publish.

Reuse happens at the level of an individual claim, not of a file (see "Changing scale").
Verification becomes collective, measurable, and continuous,
with a number of participants that manual review cannot reach (see "Measurable consensus").
A corpus is also no longer locked in its medium.
Discussions from letters, forums, and oral traditions are ported into the graph
and verified in it again (see "Authorship").

Together, this makes it possible to process the accumulated corpus again.
For example, one can evaluate quantitatively what Buddhism and Advaita have in common.
The evaluation finds where they call the same phenomenon by different names (`same`) and where they differ.
One can do this without the bottleneck of a single expert:
the evaluation accumulates from many signers.
After such knowledge is recorded, it stays addressable,
and so it is available to process further.
When new facts appear,
the corpus is processed again from the start
— now by agents (see "Agents over the graph").
Concepts are then connected to quantitative metrics,
and this improves discovery (the search for relevant information).
The graph does not compute "the same thing said differently" as similarity,
but carries it as a judgment
— the same `same` edge.
Someone states the equivalence,
so, like any edge, it can be denied (`deny_edge`) and measured by consensus.

### A worked example: what did the Buddha actually say? [~](particles/QmQYQVcyj6tns5usybsGL6NDLuuvrBqUqU5UBJALjy1LYc.md)

The Buddha wrote nothing.
He taught for approximately forty-five years, and then he died.
For four to four and a half centuries, people transmitted his words by communal recitation aloud
— and kept them in memory, not in writing.
There was no original manuscript that a person could use to verify a statement,
and no central authority with the power to certify anything.
The tradition preserved and verified his words by its own conventions, which have the same structure as the conventions above.
(The text below describes the structure that the tradition records.
People dispute whether each event occurred exactly as the tradition tells it.
The point is the form.)

**Authorship — "Thus have I heard" is a signed cyberlink.**
Almost every discourse in the canon starts with the same words.
These words are *Evaṃ me sutaṃ*, "Thus have I heard".
By tradition, these are the words of Ananda
— the attendant of the Buddha, who retells what he personally heard.
The formula does not claim authorship of the content.
It certifies provenance:
*I, this person, heard this from that source.*

```
signer = Ananda
A = hash("heard from the Buddha")     # the source / the act of hearing
B = hash(teaching)                    # the discourse, addressed by content

Ananda:  A → B                        # "Thus have I heard"
```

The signature carries the identity of Ananda.
`content(B)` is the words of the Buddha as Ananda retells them.
The formula that starts almost every discourse is the provenance edge plus the identity of the hearer:
exactly the triple that a cyberlink carries.
The special CID `hash("heard from the Buddha")` is not in the list above.
It is a convention of the tradition itself:
the set of conventions is open (see "Conventions").

The content carries more than the edge.
Every discourse starts not only with "Thus have I heard" but also with its setting.
One example:
*"On one occasion the Blessed One was dwelling near Sāvatthī, in Jeta's Grove"*.
The setting names the place, the audience, and the occasion.
Together, these are the conditions in which the words were heard.
The setting is part of `content(B)`, and it is recorded in the words of Ananda, not in the words of the Buddha.
The standing of Ananda is recorded too.
The tradition remembers him as the foremost of those who heard much.
A reader who knew none of this
could still read these dimensions from the account itself and weigh the account accordingly.

The depth of a plain "P said B" does not come from one edge.
It comes from who signed "P said B", when the signer signed it, and what the corpus already says about that signer.
Cybergraph shows the same for any signer.
The declaration of the signer is the signed cyberlink itself.
The context at the moment of the act is the earlier cyberlinks of the signer.
They run without gaps by account sequence,
so the record is provably complete:
no cyberlink hides between two transactions of the signer (see "Fundamentals").
Also, others made their own cyberlinks about the signer.

**No central authority — the Buddha's own convention for verification.**
The Buddha foresaw his death. He left a procedure to verify any teaching that people later attributed to him
— the *Mahāpadesa*, the "Four Great References" (DN 16; AN 4.180).
A person who receives the statement "I heard this from the Buddha" should

> "neither approve nor reject" it on the ground of who says it;
> instead, "having carefully memorized those words and phrases, you should check whether they fit in the discourses (suttas) and are manifested in the discipline (vinaya).
> If they do not fit... you should reject them".
> — AN 4.180

Consistency with the corpus that already exists (the Sutta and the Vinaya — the established graph) decides validity.
The authority of the person who makes the statement does not decide it.
The same verification applies even to "I heard this face to face from the Buddha".
This is validity-through-consistency
— a read policy that is different from the reputation-weighted vote count above, and robust to it.
A statement with many confirmations still fails if it contradicts the corpus.
There is no new mechanism here:
the verification reads the corpus that already exists.
The procedure itself is one more convention of the same kind.
The teaching that defines it is published into the graph as an ordinary cyberlink.

The *Kālāma Sutta* (AN 3.65) states the principle directly:
do not rely on oral tradition, lineage, hearsay, scripture, the apparent competence of the speaker, or "the teacher is ours"
— *know for yourselves.*
Here the standing of the person who makes the statement has no part in validity at all.
Results decide, not the speaker.

The canon, then, is a graph of provenance cyberlinks.
A convention of confirmation and denial governs this graph,
and the Buddha himself published this convention into the graph.
Cybergraph is the same design with cryptographic signatures and content addressing.
So "what did the Buddha actually say" becomes a question that a person can query and cross-check against the corpus,
not a matter of faith.

Preservation is a concern that is separate from verification.
The chain stores only CIDs;
the files themselves are outside it,
and any verification
(of a quote, of consistency with the corpus)
reads content.
Until people recorded it in writing, the canon survived not by verification but by replication.
For four centuries, communal recitation kept the text in thousands of memories.
In Cybergraph, content storage (pinning) has this role.
A cyberlink is indestructible,
but it has meaning only while someone stores the files that it points to.

The dimensions that a reader uses to read a signer are open in the same way as the conventions above.
Examples are statements about the experience of the signer, references to external services, and metrics imported from them.
Each of these is itself a cyberlink
that specialized signers can recheck, confirm, or deny.
Dimensions grow through conventions, not through a new mechanism.

### Applications [~](particles/QmVes43b81h74QT6ShfwFTsniFcWHwRunZHuayRtcmL6ve.md)

| Domain | Application |
|--------|-------------|
| **Science** | The claims of a paper as particles; replication = `agree_particle`; failed replication = `disagree_particle` |
| **Journalism** | Verification of sources; several reporters who independently state one fact |
| **Law** | Terms of a contract; statements of witnesses; chains of evidence |
| **Standards** | Technical specifications; agreement of the community on conventions |
| **Education** | Tests of knowledge; review of explanations |
| **AI alignment** | AI agents interact through cyberlinks; measurable aggregation of preferences |
| **Supply chains** | Statements about a product; certification; audit logs |

Twelve conventions use one primitive:
`confirm_edge`/`confirm_link`, `deny_edge`/`deny_link`, `update_edge`, `quote_particle`, `agree_particle`, `disagree_particle`, `pro`, `con`, `question`, `answer_edge`, `author_particle`, `same`.
These conventions and edge repetition, which has no special CID of its own,
are enough to express complex collaborative workflows.
Because humans and contracts can both be signers,
the same conventions apply to the interactions of AI agents without additional infrastructure.

### Reading instructions [~](particles/QmV84XHsDJZvyM8wp8tMu1QkgJvtLyM8WDyxid1dMisTvb.md)

A CID is opaque:
when a reader looks at an edge, the reader cannot see that the source of the edge is, for example, `{"about":{"cid_from":A,"cid_to":B},"op":"update_edge"}`.
A reader can recognize instructions in two ways.
The first way is to compute candidates in advance.
The reader computes the derived CIDs of everything that it already saw
— edges and cyberlinks.
Then the reader compares the sources and targets of new edges with these CIDs.
The second way is to dereference the source and read its content.
This way works only if the preimage of the derived CID
— the record itself, for example `{"about":"Qm…","op":"update_edge"}\n` —
is published as a file.
Thus, with each instruction, also publish the preimages of its derived CIDs.
The implementation of the format in Nushell, nu-cybergraph, publishes each such record itself.
If the parts of an edge do not parse by these rules, the edge is not an instruction but an ordinary edge.
The converse is not true:
a file with the bytes `{"op":"deny_edge"}\n` also exists outside the convention
— anyone can publish it.
Thus, a parse shows that an edge reads as an instruction,
but not that someone intended it as an instruction.

The two ways show the current stage of development of the cybergraph and of applications.
nu-cybergraph already implements the second way.
For the first way, a reader can compute a candidate by hand,
but there is no automatic comparison yet.
It is important that the cybergraph already gives verifiable proof of publication (see "Fundamentals").
Extensions can help to index the graph.
An example is a list of all raw CIDs that are used in the derived CIDs at the ends of an edge. In this example, the list is in the transaction memo (today the memo is functionally unused).
The list makes recognition easier,
but the edge itself still carries the instruction.

`{"op":"convention"} → {"op":"deny_edge"}` is a convention declaration.
This edge from a shared root publishes the special CID of the convention into the graph.
The description of the convention is ordinary content, and another edge connects it to the same special CID.
A reader who knows the root computes one hash and reads the outgoing edges
— the list of declared conventions.
Neither of the two ways above gives such a list.
The first person who documented the declaration of conventions in the graph was @snedashkovsky
— from the semantic convention root (see "Acknowledgement").
The root does not close the set, and it does not appoint a registry.
A declaration is a convention like any other.
Anyone who needs their own list
selects their own root and interprets it in their own way.
Like any edge, a declaration is repeated, confirmed with `confirm_edge`, and denied with `deny_edge`.

### Appendix: Cybergraph format [~](particles/QmQ82B67SkUE1BUxbMrBks62m7fQH6naB24HgHrHv7k92t.md)

A CID is always CID v0.
The content is wrapped into a standard IPFS block (UnixFS dag-pb) and hashed with sha2-256,
and the hash is encoded in base58.
The result is a string of the form Qm….
`ipfs add --cid-version=0` gives the same result.

The signer (the `signer` field in the record) is the CID of the public key of the signer.
Here the format is different from Bostrom on purpose.
Bostrom records the signer as a `bostrom1…` address (see "Fundamentals").
The format uses the CID of the key
so that the signer is addressable like any other CID.
There must be only one encoding of the signer.
If one signer can have different encodings (an address, a bare key),
the same act gets different derived CIDs of the cyberlink.
The addressing of the act breaks:
`deny_link`, the revocation of a signer's own cyberlink, the denial of the agreement of a specific signer.
Edges that are computed from different encodings of the signer target different CIDs,
and their repetitions do not match in aggregation.
The calculation of weights also suffers:
one signer under two encodings reads as two signers.
The address is a hash of the key, and the key cannot be computed from the address.
For the conversion, the key is taken from the transaction that carries the cyberlink.
The transaction publishes the key of the signer next to the signature.
Then the CID of the key is computed.
Without this step, the derived CID of a Bostrom cyberlink cannot be computed.

A derived CID is always the CID v0 of the canonical bytes of a record.
The record is flat, and all values are strings:
a nested record is first replaced by its CID.
Keys are sorted by the order of their UTF-8 bytes.
For the keys of this issue, the order is `about`, `cid_from`, `cid_to`, `op`, `signer`.
The JSON is compact: it has no whitespace outside strings,
and it ends with exactly one `\n`.
The command `printf '<record>\n' | ipfs add --only-hash --cid-version=0` reproduces any derived CID.
In the records of this issue, capital letters (A, B, X, Qm1…), `signer` and `hash("…")` stand for CIDs.
In the bytes, they are quoted strings.
There are two special cases:
the record of the edge address is `{"cid_from":A,"cid_to":B}`;
the record of the derived CID of the cyberlink is `{"cid_from":A,"cid_to":B,"signer":signer}`.
The encoding must be the same for everyone.
If it is not, the same act gets different derived CIDs, as with the two encodings of the signer above.
